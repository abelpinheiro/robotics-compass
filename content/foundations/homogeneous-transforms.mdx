## Intuition

Up until now, we have solved spatial mapping in two separate pieces.
In the section of vector and frames, we learned how to handle translation using vector addition: $^AP = ^BP + ^AP_{BORG}$.
In the section of rotations in 3D, we learned how to handle rotation using matrix multiplication: $^AP = ^{A}_{B}R \, ^BP$.

In the real world, however, a robot's end-effector is almost always both translated and rotated relative to its base. If we try to combine our two equations, we get:

$$^AP = ^{A}_{B}R \, ^BP + ^AP_{BORG}$$

While this equation is mathematically correct, it is computationally annoying. It requires two different operations: a multiplication followed by an addition. If we have a robot arm with 6 joints, chaining these equations together becomes a massive algebraic nightmare.

To help with this problem, we introduce a technique called **Homogeneous Coordinates**
## Formal definition

### The $4\times4$ matrix
A Homogeneous Transformation Matrix, usually denoted as $T$, is a $4 \times 4$ matrix that belongs to the Special Euclidean Group, or $SE(3)$. It perfectly describes a complete frame $\{B\}$ relative to frame $\{A\}$ by nesting the $3 \times 3$ rotation matrix $R$ and the $3 \times 1$ position vector $P$ inside it:

$$
    ^{A}_{B}T = \begin{bmatrix} ^{A}_{B}R & ^AP_{BORG} \\ 0 \quad 0 \quad 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}
$$

- The top-left $3 \times 3$ block is the Rotation.

- The top-right $3 \times 1$ column is the Translation (Position).

- The bottom row is $[0, 0, 0, 1]$.

### The Unified Mapping Equation
The equation has been simplified to a single multiplication. $P$ from $\{B\}$ to $\{A\}$:

$$
    ^AP = ^{A}_{B}T \, ^BP
$$

### Inverse of a homogeneous transform

Unlike pure rotation matrices, $T^{-1}$ is not equal to $T^T$. However, because $T$ is made of rigid body components, there is a computationally cheap shortcut to find the inverse without doing heavy linear algebra:

$$
    \left( ^{A}_{B}T \right)^{-1} = ^{B}_{A}T = \begin{bmatrix} R^T & -R^T P \\ 0 \quad 0 \quad 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}
$$

## Worked example

Consider a mobile robot. The global frame $\{A\}$ is the room's origin. The robot's chassis $\{B\}$ is located at $x = 3m$, $y = 2m$ on the floor and is rotated $90^\circ$ around the Z-axis (Yaw).

Mounted on the chassis is a laser scanner. In the chassis' local frame $\{B\}$, a wall is detected at $[1, 0, 0]^T$ (1 meter straight ahead of the scanner).

Let's find the global position of the wall ($^AP$) using a Homogeneous Transformation Matrix.

First, we construct the $4 \times 4$ matrix $^{A}_{B}T$.
The rotation is a $90^\circ$ Yaw ($R_z$), and the translation is $^AP_{BORG} = [3, 2, 0]^T$.

$$
    ^{A}_{B}T = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -1 & 0 & 3 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 & 2 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}
$$

Next, we prepare our local vector $^BP$ by appending a 1 to make it homogeneous:


$$
    ^BP_{hom} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}
$$

Finally, we multiply:


$$
^AP = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -1 & 0 & 3 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 & 2 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} (0\cdot1) + 3\cdot1 \\ (1\cdot1) + 2\cdot1 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 3 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}
$$

We conclude that the wall's global coordinates are $x = 3, y = 3, z = 0$.
