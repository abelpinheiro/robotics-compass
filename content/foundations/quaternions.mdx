## Intuition

Instead of relying in Euler Angles and Rotation Matrices to describe 3D rotations, modern robotics and game engines use *Quaternions*. Instead of thinking about three separate rotations (X, then Y, then Z) and using 9 different numbers to represent an orientation, we can simplify by defining this single axis and rotating the robot by a certain angle around it.

A quaternion is simply a compact, 4-number object that encodes this axis-angle concept.

## Formal definition

### The Structure of a Quaternion

A quaternion $q$ is an extension of complex numbers, but instead of one imaginary part, it has three ($i, j, k$). For robotics, we usually write a unit quaternion as a 4D vector composed of a scalar part ($w$) and a vector part ($x, y, z$):

$$
\mathbf{q} = \begin{bmatrix} w \\ x \\ y \\ z \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \cos(\theta/2) \\ v_x \sin(\theta/2) \\ v_y \sin(\theta/2) \\ v_z \sin(\theta/2) \end{bmatrix}
$$

Where:

- $\theta$ is the angle of rotation.

- $[v_x, v_y, v_z]^T$ is the unit vector representing the axis of rotation.

The quaternion must be a unit quaternion. This means its magnitude must equal 1:

$$\vert{}\vert{}q\vert{}\vert{} = \sqrt{w^2 + x^2 + y^2 + z^2} = 1$$

### Converting to Rotation Matrices
We can convert the quaternion back to $3 \times 3$ matrices to map vectors. The conversion formula is computationally cheap and avoids trigonometric functions entirely:

$$R(q) = \begin{bmatrix}  1 - 2y^2 - 2z^2 & 2xy - 2wz & 2xz + 2wy \\ 2xy + 2wz & 1 - 2x^2 - 2z^2 & 2yz - 2wx \\ 2xz - 2wy & 2yz + 2wx & 1 - 2x^2 - 2y^2 \end{bmatrix}$$

## Worked example

Let's calculate the quaternion that represents the following orientation: We are programming a drone in PyBullet. The drone starts facing forward (aligned with the global X-axis). 
We want to rotate the drone $90^\circ$ to the left around the vertical Z-axis.

To solve this, we will define the axis and the angle.
The rotation is around the Z-axis, so our unit axis vector is $\mathbf{v} = [0, 0, 1]^T$.
The angle is $\theta = 90^\circ$. (In radians, $\theta = \pi/2$).

Now, plug these into the quaternion definition:

Scalar ($w$): $\cos(90^\circ / 2) = \cos(45^\circ) = 0.707$

X part: $0 \cdot \sin(45^\circ) = 0$

Y part: $0 \cdot \sin(45^\circ) = 0$

Z part: $1 \cdot \sin(45^\circ) = 0.707$

Conclusion:
The quaternion representing a $90^\circ$ Yaw is $\mathbf{q} = [0.707, \ 0, \ 0, \ 0.707]^T$. Notice how it perfectly fulfills the unit constraint ($0.707^2 + 0.707^2 \approx 1$). This is exactly the array format you will pass into pybullet.resetBasePositionAndOrientation().

## Common pitfalls

1. Unlike Euler angles, quaternions are not human-readable. You cannot look at $[0.2, 0.4, 0.5, 0.7]$ and visualize the posture. Trust the math, use conversion functions (like scipy.spatial.transform.Rotation) when you need to print logs for debugging, and never try to manually tweak quaternion values by hand.
