## Intuition

So far, we only studied relative to static poses: *"Where is the robot right now?"* or *"What angles do I need to reach this specific point?"*

In this section, we will take time into consideration. If a robotic arm is holding a spray gun to paint a car, just reaching the right positions isn't enough. If the robot moves too fast, the paint layer will be too thin. If it moves too slowly, the paint will drip. 

To control a robot in the real world, we must understand its motion. We need to answer: *"If my motors are spinning at a certain speed, how fast is the tool moving through the air?"* 

This is the study of **Velocity Kinematics**. Instead of mapping joint *angles* to Cartesian *positions*, we will map joint *speeds* to Cartesian *velocities*.

## Formal definition

When a rigid body (like a robot's link) moves through space, its motion is entirely described by two distinct components: **Linear Velocity** and **Angular Velocity**. 

Because a robot is made of multiple moving links, we must be extremely precise about **where** we are measuring the velocity and in **which frame** we are expressing it. We will use Craig's notation to keep track of this.

### 1. Linear Velocity ($v$) and Frame Notation
Linear velocity describes how fast a specific point is moving along a straight line. If we have a position vector $P$ describing a point $Q$ relative to a frame $\{A\}$, its linear velocity is the time derivative of that position:

$$
{}^{A}v_{Q} = \frac{d}{dt}\,\left({}^{A}P_{Q}\right)
$$

Here the superscript $A$ carries **two** meanings at once: the frame the velocity is *differentiated with respect to* **and** the frame it is *expressed in*. In Craig's notation a single superscript signals that these two frames coincide.


Now suppose you already know a velocity in one frame and want the **same physical vector written in another frame's axes**. You rotate its coordinates by the rotation matrix. 
$$
{}^{A}\!\left({}^{B}v\right) = {}^{A}_{B}R \, {}^{B}v
$$

> You read the equation above as "differentiated in $\{B\}$, written/expressed in $\{A\}$'s axes". This is *not* ${}^{A}v$ ("differentiated in $\{A\}$"). The two are equal **only when ${}^{A}_{B}R$ is constant** (when $\{B\}$ does not rotate relative to $\{A\}$)

### 2. Angular Velocity ($\omega$)
Angular velocity describes how fast a body is rotating and around which axis. It is represented as a $3 \times 1$ vector denoted by $\omega$.
*   **The direction** of $\omega$ indicates the axis of rotation (determined by the right-hand rule).
*   **The magnitude** of $||\omega||$ represents the rotational speed (rad/s).

Just like linear velocity, angular velocity must be tied to a reference frame. $^A \omega_B$ means *"the angular velocity of frame $\{B\}$ relative to, and expressed in, frame $\{A\}$"*. We can also rotate angular velocity vectors between frames:
$$
^A \omega = ^A_B R \cdot ^B \omega
$$

### 3. Velocity of a Point on a Rotating Body
If a link is rotating with angular velocity $\omega$, and a point on that link is located at a position vector $r$ relative to the center of rotation, the linear velocity $v$ of that point is given by the **cross product**:

$$
v = \omega \times r
$$
This equation states that the linear velocity is always perpendicular to both the axis of rotation and the radius vector.

### 3b. General Case: Simultaneous Linear and Angular Velocity

Sections 1 and 3 are two special cases of a single formula. When frame $\{B\}$ both **translates** and **rotates** relative to $\{A\}$, and a point $Q$ may also be moving *within* $\{B\}$, the velocity of $Q$ seen from $\{A\}$ is the sum of three contributions:

$$
{}^{A}V_{Q} = \underbrace{{}^{A}V_{B\text{ORG}}}_{\text{origin translation}} + \underbrace{{}^{A}_{B}R\,{}^{B}V_{Q}}_{\text{motion within }\{B\}} + \underbrace{{}^{A}\Omega_{B} \times {}^{A}_{B}R\,{}^{B}Q}_{\text{induced by rotation}} \tag{Craig 5.13}
$$

where ${}^{A}V_{B\text{ORG}}$ is the linear velocity of the **origin** of $\{B\}$ through $\{A\}$, ${}^{B}V_{Q}$ is how $Q$ moves *as measured inside* $\{B\}$, and ${}^{A}\Omega_{B}$ is the angular velocity of $\{B\}$ relative to $\{A\}$.

![A fixed world frame A and a moving frame B whose origin translates with velocity A-V-Borg (blue) and rotates with angular velocity A-Omega-B (orange). A point Q sits at position r inside B. The velocity of Q seen from A is the tip-to-tail sum of three contributions at Q: the origin translation (blue), the motion of Q within B re-expressed in A (green), and the rotation-induced term Omega cross r, perpendicular to r (orange). Their vector sum is the total A-V-Q (pink).](/images/kinematics/velocity-kinematics-general-velocity.svg)

Two special cases recover what we saw earlier:

- If ${}^{A}_{B}R$ is **constant** (no relative rotation), the last term vanishes and we recover the pure re-expression from Section 1.
- If the origins coincide and $Q$ is **fixed in $\{B\}$** (so ${}^{B}V_{Q}=0$), only the rotation term survives:
$$
{}^{A}V_{Q} = {}^{A}\Omega_{B} \times {}^{A}Q \tag{Craig 5.10}
$$
which is exactly the cross-product rule $v = \omega \times r$ from Section 3.

**Angular velocities add.** Unlike linear velocity, angular velocity is a *pure* free vector: that means that it has no point of application, so re-expressing it is always just a rotation (there is no $\omega \times r$ analogue for it). This is why, once written in a common frame, angular velocities simply add:

$$
{}^{A}\omega_{C} = {}^{A}\omega_{B} + {}^{A}_{B}R\,{}^{B}\omega_{C}
$$

This additivity is the key that makes velocity propagation from link to link possible, because each joint contributes its own angular velocity, and they accumulate down the chain.

### 4. The Derivative of a Rotation Matrix
How does angular velocity relate to the rotation matrices ($R$) we used in Forward Kinematics? 
If a frame $\{B\}$ is rotating relative to frame $\{A\}$, its rotation matrix $^A_B R$ is changing over time. The derivative of this matrix over time ($\dot{R}$) is mathematically tied to the angular velocity.

The skew-symmetric matrix $S(\omega)$ is just the cross product $S(\omega)\,r = \omega \times r$. Rewriting it this way is useful because a matrix composes cleanly with the rotation matrices $R$, which a simple cross product does not.

To express this without cross products, we convert the $3 \times 1$ angular velocity vector $\omega = [\omega_x, \omega_y, \omega_z]^T$ into a $3 \times 3$ **skew-symmetric matrix** denoted as $S(\omega)$:

$$
S(\omega) = \begin{bmatrix} 
0 & -\omega_z & \omega_y \\ 
\omega_z & 0 & -\omega_x \\ 
-\omega_y & \omega_x & 0 
\end{bmatrix}
$$

Using this matrix, the time derivative of a rotation matrix is defined as:
$$
^A_B \dot{R} = S(^A \omega_B) \cdot ^A_B R
$$
The equation above says the rate of change of a body's orientation is its current orientation **pre-multiplied by the skew-symmetric operator of its angular velocity**.

### Velocity Propagation from Link to Link
Differentiating the entire Forward Kinematics matrix ($^0_nT$) to find the end-effector's velocity results in a massive algebraic equation. Instead, we can find it by computing velocities recursively.
To propagate velocities from frame ${i}$ to frame ${i+1}$, we use the rotation matrices $^{i+1}_iR$ and position matrices $^{i}P_{i+1}$

#### For revolute joints
A revolute joint adds angular velocity around the $Z$-axis, but adds no independent linear sliding.

1. **Angular velocity**: The angular velocity of link $i+1$ is the angular velocity of link $i$ (rotated into the new frame) plus the new rotation from the motor 

$$
{}^{i+1}\omega_{i+1}
=
{}^{i+1}_{i}R \, {}^{i}\omega_i
+
\begin{bmatrix}
0 \\
0 \\
\dot{\theta}_{i+1}
\end{bmatrix}
$$

2. **Linear Velocity**: The linear velocity of the origin of frame $\{i+1\}$ is the linear velocity of frame $\{i\}$'s origin, plus the tangential velocity caused by frame $\{i\}$ rotating the new origin through space (the $\omega \times r$ cross product we defined earlier). The whole result is then rotated into frame

$$
{}^{i+1}v_{i+1}
=
{}^{i+1}_{i}R
\left(
{}^{i}v_i
+
{}^{i}\omega_i \times {}^{i}P_{i+1}
\right)
$$

#### For a Prismatic Joint
A prismatic joint adds linear velocity along the $Z$-axis, but adds no rotation.

1. **Angular Velocity**: Because the joint does not rotate, link $i+1$ shares the exact same angular velocity as link $i$. We only need to rotate the vector into the new frame.

$$
^{i+1}\omega_{i+1} = ^{i+1}_iR \, ^i\omega_i
$$

2. **Linear Velocity**: The linear velocity calculation is identical to the revolute case, but we add the physical sliding speed of the joint $\dot{d}_{i+1}$ along the new $Z$-axis.

$$
^{i+1}v_{i+1} = ^{i+1}_iR \left( ^iv_i + ^i\omega_i \times ^iP_{i+1} \right) + \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ \dot{d}_{i+1} \end{bmatrix}
$$

## Worked example: Propagating Velocities on a 2-DOF Arm
Let's calculate the velocity of the end-effector for a 2-DOF planar robot (link lengths $L_1$ and $L_2$) moving with joint speeds $\dot{\theta}_1$ and $\dot{\theta}_2$. We will use the recursive propagation method from the base $\{0\}$ to the tool frame $\{3\}$.

**1. Base to Link 1**
The base does not move, so $^0\omega_0 = 0$ and $^0v_0 = 0$. 
Joint 1 is a revolute joint rotating around the Z-axis. Since frame $\{0\}$ and $\{1\}$ share the same origin in our DH setup ($^0P_1 = [0,0,0]^T$), the linear velocity remains zero.
$$
^1\omega_1 = ^1_0R \, ^0\omega_0 + \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ \dot{\theta}_1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ \dot{\theta}_1 \end{bmatrix}
$$
$$
^1v_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}
$$

**2. Link 1 to Link 2**
Frame $\{2\}$ is translated by $L_1$ along the X-axis of frame $\{1\}$, so $^1P_2 = [L_1, 0, 0]^T$. The rotation matrix $^2_1R$ is a standard Z-axis rotation by $\theta_2$.

First, we propagate the angular velocity. Since the Z-axes are always parallel in planar robots, the Z-component passes through directly:
$$
^2\omega_2 = ^2_1R \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ \dot{\theta}_1 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ \dot{\theta}_2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ \dot{\theta}_1 + \dot{\theta}_2 \end{bmatrix}
$$

Next, the linear velocity. We take the cross product of $^1\omega_1$ and $^1P_2$:
$$
^1\omega_1 \times ^1P_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ \dot{\theta}_1 \end{bmatrix} \times \begin{bmatrix} L_1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ L_1 \dot{\theta}_1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}
$$
Now we rotate this into frame $\{2\}$ using $^2_1R$:
$$
^2v_2 = \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta_2 & \sin\theta_2 & 0 \\ -\sin\theta_2 & \cos\theta_2 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ L_1 \dot{\theta}_1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} L_1 \dot{\theta}_1 \sin\theta_2 \\ L_1 \dot{\theta}_1 \cos\theta_2 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}
$$

**3. Link 2 to the End-Effector (Frame 3)**
The tool is fixed at the end of Link 2, so $^2P_3 = [L_2, 0, 0]^T$ and there is no new joint rotation ($^3_2R$ is the identity matrix, and $\dot{\theta}_3 = 0$).
$$
^3\omega_3 = ^2\omega_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ \dot{\theta}_1 + \dot{\theta}_2 \end{bmatrix}
$$
We compute the cross product $^2\omega_2 \times ^2P_3$:
$$
\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ \dot{\theta}_1 + \dot{\theta}_2 \end{bmatrix} \times \begin{bmatrix} L_2 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ L_2(\dot{\theta}_1 + \dot{\theta}_2) \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}
$$
Adding this to the linear velocity of frame 2 ($^2v_2$) gives the final linear velocity of the tool, expressed entirely in its own local frame:
$$
^3v_3 = \begin{bmatrix} L_1 \dot{\theta}_1 \sin\theta_2 \\ L_1 \dot{\theta}_1 \cos\theta_2 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ L_2(\dot{\theta}_1 + \dot{\theta}_2) \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} L_1 \dot{\theta}_1 \sin\theta_2 \\ L_1 \dot{\theta}_1 \cos\theta_2 + L_2(\dot{\theta}_1 + \dot{\theta}_2) \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}
$$

## Common pitfalls
1. **Adding Vectors from Different Frames:** The single most common math error in velocity propagation is attempting to add a vector expressed in Frame $i$ directly to a vector expressed in Frame $i+1$. You must *always* pre-multiply by the rotation matrix $^{i+1}_iR$ before adding.
2. **Cross Product Ordering:** The cross product is anti-commutative. This means $v = \omega \times r$ is different from $r \times \omega$. Reversing the order flips the sign of the resulting velocity vector, indicating that the math that your robot is moving backward.
